\chapter{Robot systems and sensing}
\label{chap:lecture06}

Reference frames and transformations describe where a robot and the objects
around it are located. A robot must also obtain information about those objects,
decide what to do, and produce physical motion. These activities connect the
geometric tools of the preceding chapters to the operation of a robot system.

\section{Sensing, processing, and action}
A useful working description of a robot is an intelligent interconnection
between sensing and action. Sensors collect measurements; computation uses
those measurements to select actions; actuators carry out the actions.
The resulting interaction with the environment produces new measurements,
closing a feedback loop.

\section{Sensors and the environment}
The \emph{environment} includes the objects, surfaces, and people with which
the robot interacts. A camera measures incoming light, a microphone responds
to pressure variations, and a contact sensor detects physical contact.
Sensors may also measure the robot's own state; sensing is not limited to
observing external objects.

A measurement is not yet an interpretation of the scene. An image records
light arriving at the camera; identifying an object and estimating its
location require processing that image. Likewise, a contact measurement
indicates an interaction at a surface but does not by itself describe the
entire surrounding space. Each type of sensor provides particular information
that the robot must interpret in relation to its task.

The coordinate transformations developed earlier enter at this point. An
object located in a sensor frame may need to be expressed in a robot frame
to determine whether it is within reach, or in a world frame to plan motion.
The physical object is the same; the coordinate description is chosen to
support the calculation being performed.

\section{Actuators and physical interaction}
\emph{Actuators} produce physical action, for example by driving wheels or
moving a manipulator. Wheel-driven motion depends on forces exchanged with
the supporting surface. An action may change the environment, the robot's
pose relative to it, or both.

The distinction between sensing and action is a useful functional one:
sensors provide measurements, whereas actuators produce physical effects.
A motor command can turn a wheel or move a joint, but the resulting motion
also depends on the mechanism and its interaction with the surroundings.
Moving a mobile robot changes what its sensors observe, even when the objects
in the environment remain fixed in the world frame.

\section{Connecting measurements to commands}
The connection between sensing and action includes both computing hardware
and software. It may involve interpreting measurements, estimating where
objects are, selecting a plan, and producing individual actuator commands.
Here, ``intelligent'' does not require a neural network or machine learning:
explicit algorithms and feedback control can also provide this connection.

The central block in Figure~\ref{fig:l06-components} therefore represents
several possible levels of computation. A high-level decision might be to
approach an object. More detailed decisions specify how to move, and the
commands sent to the actuators must ultimately specify actions that the
hardware can execute. All of these functions belong to the connection
between measurements and physical action, even when they run in different
programs or on different processors.

The enclosing boundary in the diagram is also important: the robot includes
the sensors and actuators as well as the computing hardware and software.
The central block alone is not the entire robot. The arrows trace a loop
through the environment: an action changes the physical situation, sensors
measure the resulting situation, and computation can use those measurements
to decide the next action.

\subsection{Example: reaching an object}
Suppose a robot must grasp an object that is initially beyond its reach.
Sensor measurements first support an estimate of the object's location.
The robot must then determine that it needs to move closer, choose an approach,
and command its motion before extending the manipulator and closing the gripper.
A high-level objective thus becomes a sequence of physical actions, with
measurements available to check progress and adjust the commands.

Consider the distinction between the decisions involved. Recognizing the
object answers what the robot should interact with. Estimating its position
answers where it is. Comparing that position with the robot's reach determines
whether the base must move first. Planning selects a sequence of motions,
and control turns those motions into actuator commands. These decisions
serve the same task but require different information and computations.

After the robot approaches, the object's coordinates relative to the robot
have changed. New measurements can update its estimated location before the
grasp. This illustrates why sensing and action are connected repeatedly:
the robot does not merely observe once and then execute an unrelated motion.

\section{Proprioceptive and exteroceptive sensing}
Sensors can be classified by the quantities they measure.
\emph{Proprioceptive} sensors provide information about the robot itself,
such as wheel rotation or joint position. A vehicle's speed indication is
an example of information about its own motion. \emph{Exteroceptive} sensors
provide information about the environment or the robot's relationship to it,
such as the distance to an obstacle. A parking-distance sensor illustrates
this second category. The distinction concerns the measured quantity, not
whether the sensor is physically mounted inside or outside the robot.

The following sections develop general measurement concepts and then focus
on exteroceptive distance sensing. These measurements help a mobile robot
detect obstacles and express their locations in the reference frames used
for planning and control.

\section{From a physical quantity to a measurement}
A sensor system converts a physical quantity into information available to
software. For a force measurement, a typical chain is
\[
 F\longrightarrow\text{electrical signal}\longrightarrow
 \text{ADC code}\longrightarrow\bar F.
\]
Here $F$ is the actual force and $\bar F$ is the reported force in physical
units. The analog-to-digital converter (ADC) produces a number representing
an input voltage; calibration relates that number to the force.

Different transducers use different physical effects. A conventional
resistive strain gauge changes resistance when deformed; a capacitive
transducer instead uses a change in capacitance. Signal conditioning turns
the relevant electrical change into a suitable input for measurement.
Both mechanisms can support force measurement, but use different electrical
effects.\footnote{See
\href{https://www.ni.com/en/shop/data-acquisition/sensor-fundamentals/measuring-strain-with-strain-gages.html}{NI, Measuring Strain with Strain Gages}.}

\subsection{Quantization and conversion to physical units}
An 8-bit ADC has $2^8=256$ possible codes, numbered 0 through 255.
For an ideal uniform converter spanning 0 to $5\,\mathrm V$, the nominal
voltage step is
\[
 \Delta V=\frac{5}{256}\,\mathrm V\approx19.5\,\mathrm{mV}.
\]
With the illustrative rule $k=\lfloor V/\Delta V\rfloor$ for
$0\le V<5\,\mathrm V$, an input of $2.31\,\mathrm V$ gives code 118.
The exact transition convention depends on the converter, but the principle
is unchanged: a finite interval of input voltages maps to one code.
The code is not itself a force in newtons. Software must apply the calibrated
relationship between the transducer output and the physical quantity.

Quantization, electrical disturbances, and variation in the physical
measurement process can all contribute to the difference between $F$ and
$\bar F$. Increasing the number of digital bits alone does not eliminate
all of these errors.

\section{Bias, noise, and calibration}
Within a useful operating interval, a simple measurement model is
\begin{equation}
 \bar F_k=F_k+b+\nu_k,
 \label{eq:l06-noise}
\end{equation}
where $b$ is a constant bias and $\nu_k$ is the noise in sample $k$.
This model assumes the scale factor has already been set correctly.
The ideal measurement has $b=0$ and $\nu_k=0$. A bias of $0.5\,\mathrm N$
shifts every measurement upward by $0.5\,\mathrm N$; noise causes readings
to vary even when the applied force is held fixed.

After bias correction, individual samples can still fluctuate about the
true value. The time trace is schematic, not experimental data.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Estimating a constant offset}
Calibration compares measurements with known reference inputs. Suppose a
constant reference force $F_0$ is applied and $M$ samples are collected.
Under the constant-bias model with zero-mean noise, an estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat b=\frac1M\sum_{k=1}^M\bar F_k-F_0,
 \qquad F_{\mathrm{corr}}=\bar F-\widehat b.
 \label{eq:l06-calibration}
\end{equation}
For this model, averaging is also the least-squares estimate of the constant
offset. It is not a universal calibration procedure for every sensor.

For $F_0=5\,\mathrm N$ and readings $4.93$, $4.95$, and
$4.91\,\mathrm N$, the mean is $4.93\,\mathrm N$, so
\[
 \widehat b=-0.07\,\mathrm N.
\]
Subtracting this bias means adding $0.07\,\mathrm N$ to a measurement.
The corrected samples are $5.00$, $5.02$, and $4.98\,\mathrm N$:
their mean agrees with the reference, but the individual values still vary.
One sample alone cannot distinguish its random error from a constant offset.

For independent noise samples with equal variance $\sigma^2$, the variance
of their mean is $\sigma^2/M$. Thus averaging reduces uncertainty in the
offset estimate under those assumptions. Correlation or drift can make
averaging less effective, and calibration does not remove future noise.

\subsection{Limits of a simple calibration model}
If the response also has an unknown gain, a model such as
$\bar F=aF+b+\nu$ requires reference measurements at more than one force.
A nonlinear response may require fitting a curve over the intended operating
range. A sufficiently smooth response can often be approximated locally by
a line, but that does not justify extrapolating the line indefinitely.

Sensors have physical operating limits. A compression-only sensing assembly
need not measure tensile loading, while other force sensors can measure both
directions. At large inputs, a device may saturate or be damaged. Calibration
must therefore specify its applicable range and conditions; it cannot make
an unsupported input measurable.

\section{Distance sensing with ultrasound}
A mobile robot needs information about obstacles early enough to respond.
An ultrasonic range sensor emits a sound pulse and detects a returning echo.
If $v_s$ is the sound speed in the medium and $\Delta t$ is the round-trip
travel time, then
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{d=\frac{v_s\Delta t}{2}.}
 \label{eq:l06-sonar}
\end{equation}
The factor two accounts for travel to the obstacle and back. This approximation
treats the emitter and receiver as colocated relative to the target distance
and assumes the detected echo follows the intended path.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering\input{figures/lecture06/sonar}
\caption{Ultrasonic ranging measures the outgoing and return travel time.
Emitter--receiver separation is exaggerated for clarity.}
\label{fig:l06-sonar}
\end{figure}

For example, using $v_s=343\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and a measured delay of
$10\,\mathrm{ms}$ gives $d=1.715\,\mathrm m$. The sound speed must match
the medium and conditions; the same assumed value cannot be used for both
air and water.

\subsection{Weak echoes, multipath, and interference}
The acoustic wave spreads as it propagates, and only part of the reflected
energy returns to the receiver. Absorption and target geometry further affect
the echo. A weak or missing return can therefore limit useful range; it does
not necessarily establish that no obstacle exists.

An echo may also follow a longer path involving several reflecting surfaces.
This \emph{multipath} return corresponds to total path length, not necessarily
twice the direct distance to the intended obstacle. With several ultrasonic
sensors nearby, one receiver may detect another transmitter's pulse,
creating \emph{crosstalk}.

One mitigation is to trigger sensors in sequence and allow earlier echoes
to decay. The waiting interval depends on the sensor and environment; there
is no universal delay of several seconds. For a maximum intended range
$d_{\max}$, $2d_{\max}/v_s$ is the direct round-trip travel time, to which
practical timing allowances may need to be added. Sequential operation also
means that a set of readings is collected over time rather than instantaneously.

\section{Infrared reflection and laser ranging}
An infrared emitter and receiver can detect reflected light from an object.
In a simple intensity-based sensor, received signal strength is related to
distance through calibration. The difficulty is that signal strength also
depends on the target's reflectance, orientation, and other conditions.
A less reflective target may therefore be interpreted as farther away if
the calibration assumes a more reflective target.

Visible color alone does not determine reflectance at an infrared wavelength.
The relevant property is the material's response to the emitted light.
At approximately fixed height, an array of downward-facing reflectance
sensors can use differences between a line and its background for line
following. In that application, contrast is the desired information rather
than an unwanted influence on a distance estimate.

Infrared describes a wavelength band, not a unique ranging method.
Optical time-of-flight ranging is also possible with suitable electronics;
it is not ruled out by the high speed of light.\footnote{For an example of
infrared time-of-flight technology, see
\href{https://www.st.com/en/applications/sensing/proximity-ranging-sensing.html}{STMicroelectronics, Proximity Sensing Applications}.}
Laser rangefinders can use pulse travel time, phase-based techniques, or
other ranging principles. A laser beam is narrow but still has finite
divergence, and a useful return depends on the target and receiver.

\subsection{Why scan the beam?}
A single narrow beam measures along one direction. An obstacle outside that
direction can remain undetected even when the reported distance is correct.
A planar laser scanner samples multiple directions, often by rotating a
beam or optical assembly. It associates each measured range with a bearing,
producing a scan of the surrounding geometry in a plane.

Coverage is limited by the field of view, angular sampling, minimum and
maximum usable range, and whether the obstacle intersects the scan plane.
Scanning also takes time. A robot or object that moves during acquisition
may change position between the first and last readings.

\section{Representing a planar laser scan}
Let $S$ be the sensor frame, with bearings measured counterclockwise from
$x_S$ toward $y_S$. A scan contains ranges
\[
 \boldsymbol r=\begin{bmatrix}r_1&r_2&\cdots&r_N\end{bmatrix}\trans,
\]
together with the information needed to determine each ray's angle and
interpret its validity. Suppose $N\ge2$ uniformly spaced rays include both
endpoints $\gamma_{\min}$ and $\gamma_{\max}$. Define the field of view
$\alpha$ and angular spacing $\beta$ by
\begin{equation}
 \alpha=\gamma_{\max}-\gamma_{\min},\qquad
 \boxed{\beta=\frac{\alpha}{N-1}},\qquad
 \boxed{\gamma_i=\gamma_{\min}+(i-1)\beta}.
 \label{eq:l06-angles}
\end{equation}
There are $N-1$ intervals between $N$ endpoint-inclusive samples. In
particular, $\gamma_1=\gamma_{\min}$ and $\gamma_N=\gamma_{\max}$.
A full-circle scan that omits a duplicate endpoint uses a different counting
convention; always follow the device's actual angular metadata.

obstacle points; dashed rays illustrate directions without a depicted return

\label{fig:l06-scan}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Worked example: 241 rays}
For $\gamma_{\min}=-2\pi/3$, $\gamma_{\max}=2\pi/3$, and $N=241$,
\[
 \alpha=\frac{4\pi}{3}=240^\circ,\qquad
 \beta=\frac{4\pi/3}{240}=\frac{\pi}{180}=1^\circ.
\]
Thus $\gamma_i=-120^\circ+(i-1)1^\circ$. Ray 1 points at
$-120^\circ$, ray 121 at $0^\circ$, and ray 241 at $120^\circ$.
The indices here start at one; an array indexed from zero instead uses
$\gamma_j=\gamma_{\min}+j\beta$ for $j=0,\ldots,N-1$.

Angular spacing is distinct from range resolution or measurement accuracy.
At range $r$, two directions separated by $\beta$ intersect a circle at points
separated by chord length $2r\sin(\beta/2)$. For small angles this is
approximately $r\beta$, with $\beta$ in radians. Consequently, the gaps between
sampled directions become wider with distance, and a thin obstacle can fall
between adjacent rays.

\subsection{Valid returns and missing measurements}
A range reading must be interpreted using the device's valid range and
status conventions before being converted into an obstacle point. Invalid
or missing returns may use special codes, infinity, or other representations.
Do not assume that zero or the maximum-range value always has one universal
meaning. In particular, a missing return is not an obstacle located at the
maximum range, nor proof that the whole direction is unobstructed.

As a concrete software convention, the ROS \texttt{LaserScan} message carries
angle and range metadata and specifies that ranges below its minimum or above
its maximum should be discarded.\footnote{See the official
\href{https://github.com/ros2/common_interfaces/blob/rolling/sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan.msg}{ROS LaserScan message definition}.}
The sensor and driver documentation determine the remaining status details.

\section{From ranges to obstacle coordinates}
For a valid return at range $r_i$ and bearing $\gamma_i$, the corresponding
point in the sensor frame is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{B_i^S=\begin{bmatrix}x_i^S\\y_i^S\end{bmatrix}
 =\begin{bmatrix}r_i\cos\gamma_i\\r_i\sin\gamma_i\end{bmatrix}.}
 \label{eq:l06-polar}
\end{equation}
This is the polar-to-Cartesian conversion. The inverse is
\begin{equation}
 r_i=\sqrt{(x_i^S)^2+(y_i^S)^2},\qquad
 \gamma_i=\operatorname{atan2}(y_i^S,x_i^S).
 \label{eq:l06-cartesian}
\end{equation}
At the origin, the range is zero and the bearing is geometrically undefined.

\caption{A valid range and bearing determine Cartesian coordinates in the
sensor frame. The projections give the cosine and sine components.}
\label{fig:l06-polar}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The sensor frame is not necessarily the robot frame}
A sensor rigidly attached to a robot has a fixed mounting transform, but
its axes and origin need not coincide with the robot's. In the planar case,
\begin{equation}
 B_i^R=R_S^R B_i^S+p_S^R,\qquad
 \widetilde B_i^W=H_R^W H_S^R\widetilde B_i^S.
 \label{eq:l06-frames}
\end{equation}
For a spatial mounting, embed the scan point as
$[r_i\cos\gamma_i\;r_i\sin\gamma_i\;0]\trans$ in $S$ and apply the
$4\times4$ transformations of Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture05}. A horizontal
scan in the sensor frame need not be horizontal in the world frame.

For example, ray 151 in the 241-ray scan has bearing $30^\circ$.
If $r_{151}=2\,\mathrm m$, then
\[
 B_{151}^S=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3\\1\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm m.
\]
Suppose the sensor is rotated by $90^\circ$ relative to the robot and its
origin is at $p_S^R=[0.2\;0]\trans\,\mathrm m$. Then
\[
 B_{151}^R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
 \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt3\\1\end{bmatrix}
 +\begin{bmatrix}.2\\0\end{bmatrix}
 =\begin{bmatrix}-.8\\\sqrt3\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm m.
\]
Treating the original sensor coordinates as robot coordinates would give
the wrong location even though the range measurement itself was correct.
Finally, all these conversions act on measured data: calibration error and
noise remain present in the resulting obstacle coordinates.

\section{Additional exercises}
State assumptions and units. Use the endpoint-inclusive scan convention unless
otherwise specified.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Classifying measurements.} Classify a wheel-rotation measurement,
a joint-angle measurement, and a distance to a wall as proprioceptive or
exteroceptive. Explain why the sensor's mounting location does not decide
the classification.

\item \textbf{ADC conversion.} Use the ideal 8-bit, 0--$5\,\mathrm V$ model
in this chapter. Find the code for $2.50\,\mathrm V$ and the voltage interval
represented by code 118. Why is neither number automatically a force?

\item \textbf{Offset calibration.} A $10\,\mathrm N$ reference gives readings
$9.8$, $9.9$, $9.7$, and $9.8\,\mathrm N$. Estimate the constant bias and
correct a later reading of $12.3\,\mathrm N$. State the assumptions under
which averaging is appropriate and explain why corrected readings still vary.

\item \textbf{Ultrasonic ranging.} With $v_s=343\,\mathrm{m/s}$, find the
range corresponding to a $12\,\mathrm{ms}$ echo delay. Find the direct
round-trip time for a $4\,\mathrm m$ maximum range. Explain how multipath
and a neighboring transmitter can each invalidate a naive distance estimate.

\item \textbf{Scan indexing.} A scanner samples $[-120^\circ,120^\circ]$
with 241 rays. Find the bearings of rays 1, 61, 121, and 241. Which ray
points at $45^\circ$? Repeat the last answer using zero-based indexing.

\item \textbf{Polar coordinates and mounting.} Ray 61 of the preceding scan
returns $2\,\mathrm m$. Find its sensor-frame coordinates. Transform them
to the robot frame for mounting rotation $90^\circ$ and translation
$[0.2\;0]\trans\,\mathrm m$. Recover the original range and bearing from
the sensor-frame Cartesian coordinates.

\item \textbf{What a scan cannot establish.} At range $5\,\mathrm m$, compute
the chord spacing between rays separated by $1^\circ$. Explain why a narrow
obstacle might be missed. Distinguish a blind sector, an invalid return,
and a valid detection, and explain why plotting every missing return at
$r_{\max}$ is misleading.
\end{enumerate}
